# Major-gene fixation depletes locus-specific variance but not exploitable genetic gain: a quantitative-genetics simulation study in edible maize breeding

**Author:** Nguyen Trung Duc¹, Pham Quang Tuan¹
¹ Institute of Biotechnology and Agriculture, Vietnam National University of Agriculture, Hanoi, Vietnam

## Abstract

Once a large-effect quality locus is fixed in a commercial crop market class, its own
contribution to additive genetic variance falls to zero by definition (V_A = 2*pq*·a²),
yet whether the remaining polygenic background still supports meaningful genetic gain —
and which breeding strategy exploits it best — is rarely demonstrated quantitatively.
We built a forward-time quantitative-genetics simulation (AlphaSimR) of an edible-maize
breeding population carrying one major-effect quality locus (sh2-like) and 300
background QTL. Across 20 independent replicates, sustained truncation selection fixed
the major locus by generation `3.4` ± `1.4` (mean ± SD), collapsing its own
additive variance to zero while background additive variance remained at
`0.392` — matching the classical prediction exactly. Starting from this
fixed population, we compared three post-fixation strategies over 12 further
generations: single-score phenotypic mass selection, multi-trait index selection on
four decomposed quality components, and genomic selection (GBLUP). Multi-trait index
selection produced `6.9`% more cumulative genetic gain than phenotypic mass
selection and `33.4`% more than genomic selection under a realistic,
non-accumulating training population (mean prediction accuracy r = `0.319`). These
results give the "variance-depletion by major-gene fixation" argument — increasingly
invoked qualitatively in edible-maize breeding reviews — a reproducible, numeric
demonstration, and show that how phenotyping is structured matters as much as which
prediction technology is used once a program is past its major-gene stage.

## 1. Introduction

Classical quantitative genetics predicts that once an allele at a diallelic locus
reaches fixation (p → 1 or 0), that locus's own contribution to additive genetic
variance, V_A = 2*pq*·a², falls to zero regardless of the size of its original effect
*a* (Falconer & Mackay, 1996). In several clonally- and hybrid-propagated specialty
crops, large-effect endosperm or quality mutations were fixed decades ago within their
respective commercial market classes — *sh2* and *su1* in sweet corn, *wx1* in waxy
corn — yet recent multi-omic studies continue to report substantial, still-improvable
variation in eating quality within those same market classes (Li et al. 2025a; Luo et
al. 2024; Li et al. 2025b). The qualitative explanation is straightforward: once the
major locus stops contributing variance, all remaining exploitable variation must sit
in the polygenic background. What is missing from the literature is a quantitative
demonstration of (i) how quickly this depletion actually occurs under realistic
selection pressure, (ii) how much background variance genuinely remains once it does,
and (iii) which breeding strategy converts that residual variance into gain most
efficiently. This simulation study addresses all three questions directly, as a
reproducible companion to a broader review of phenotype-centered breeding logic for
edible maize (Nguyen & Pham, 2026).

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Simulated genome and trait architecture

Founder haplotypes for `800` individuals across `10` chromosomes
(`400` segregating sites per chromosome) were generated by coalescent
simulation (`AlphaSimR::runMacs`, species = "MAIZE", inbred founders). The simulated
quality trait was controlled by two independent additive components: (1) one
major-effect locus, isolated to a single chromosome, representing a large-effect
endosperm mutation such as *sh2*; and (2) `300` background QTL
(`30` per chromosome) with small, independently drawn additive effects,
representing the polygenic quality background (pericarp thickness, organic acids,
volatile compounds, and other minor-effect loci identified in recent multi-omic
studies). An 800-marker SNP panel (`80` per chromosome) was retained for
genomic prediction, disjoint from the QTL positions. Table 1 lists all simulation
parameters.

### 2.2 Phase 1 — historical fixation

Starting from the segregating founder population, `18` generations of
truncation selection (top `20`%, random mating among selected parents) were
applied to a single composite phenotype (true additive value plus environmental noise,
heritability = `0.35`), mimicking historical selection for a commercial
quality target. Allele frequency and additive variance at the major locus, and additive
variance in the polygenic background, were recorded every generation.

### 2.3 Phase 2 — three post-fixation strategies

The final Phase 1 population (major locus fixed) was used as the common starting point
for three independent, parallel breeding strategies run for `12` further
generations each:

- **Arm A — Phenotypic mass selection.** One noisy composite phenotype per individual
  per generation (heritability = `0.35`), truncation selection at
  `20`%.
- **Arm B — Multi-trait index selection.** The true polygenic breeding value was
  decomposed into four independent sub-trait components (disjoint QTL subsets,
  representing sweetness, pericarp tenderness, aroma, and postharvest quality — the
  same four components named in the companion review), each phenotyped separately with
  less measurement noise (heritability = `0.50` per component) and combined
  via a Smith–Hazel-type index using the population's own known additive
  variance/phenotypic variance ratios per component.
- **Arm C — Genomic selection.** Genomic best linear unbiased prediction (GBLUP) using
  a VanRaden genomic relationship matrix (`AGHmatrix::Gmatrix`) fitted with
  `sommer::mmer`. A fresh random `50`% of each generation's population was
  phenotyped (same composite phenotype and heritability as Arm A) as the training set;
  the remaining candidates were ranked on GEBV. Training population accuracy was
  computed each generation as the correlation between GEBV and true breeding value in
  the held-out (untrained) individuals.

Cumulative genetic gain (change in mean true polygenic breeding value from generation 0
to generation `12`) was recorded for each arm in each replicate.

### 2.4 Replication and statistical analysis

The full Phase 1 → Phase 2 pipeline was independently repeated `20` times, each
with an independently sampled coalescent founder population (AlphaSimR's coalescent
sampler is not fully determined by R's own random seed, so independent replicates were
treated as the unit of inference rather than relying on single-run reproducibility).
Results are reported as mean ± SD across replicates. Pairwise differences in cumulative
gain between arms were tested with paired two-sided t-tests across replicates (each
replicate contributes one paired observation per arm, since all three arms in a given
replicate share the same Phase 1 starting population).

### 2.5 Software

All simulations were run in R 4.5.1 using AlphaSimR (Gaynor et al. 2021), AGHmatrix
(Amadeu et al. 2016), and sommer (Covarrubias-Pazaran 2016). Complete, executable code
is available at the repository cited in Data and Code Availability.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 The major locus fixes rapidly and its variance collapses exactly as predicted

*(Table 2, Figure 1)* Across `20` replicates, the major locus reached apparent
fixation (allele frequency ≥ 0.999) by generation `3.4` ± `1.4`
under sustained selection. Its own additive variance fell from an initial mean of
`1.001` to `0.0000` at fixation, tracking the theoretical
V_A = 2*pq*·a² curve closely at every generation (Figure 1B). Background additive
variance, by contrast, remained well above zero throughout — `0.392` at
the point of major-locus fixation, and `0.211` after `18`
total generations of continued selection.

### 3.2 Multi-trait index selection extracts the most gain from the residual background

*(Table 3, Figure 2)* Figure 2 is an eight-panel composite built entirely from the
replicated output: (A) cumulative gain by strategy; (B) paired per-replicate
trajectories across the three arms; (C, D) paired-difference distributions
(B − A, B − C); (E) genomic-prediction accuracy distribution; (F) generation-to-fixation
versus residual background variance; (G) prediction accuracy versus realized
genomic-selection gain; (H) relative advantage of Arm B with 95% confidence
intervals from the paired t-tests. Starting from the major-locus-fixed population,
cumulative genetic gain after `12` generations differed significantly between
strategies. Multi-trait index selection (Arm B) achieved `4.063` ± `0.345`,
significantly higher than phenotypic mass selection (Arm A: `3.801` ±
`0.308`; paired t-test, `t(19) = 10.10, p < 0.001`) and higher still than genomic selection
under a non-accumulating training population (Arm C: `3.046` ± `0.276`;
paired t-test, `t(19) = 28.29, p < 0.001`). Mean genomic-prediction accuracy in Arm C was
`0.319` ± `0.020` — moderate, reflecting the marker density and training
population size used here rather than a general property of genomic selection (see
Discussion).

## 4. Discussion

The simulation converts two claims that are usually stated narratively in the breeding
literature into numbers. First, that major-gene fixation genuinely does collapse a
locus's own contribution to additive variance essentially to zero, on a timescale
(single-digit generations under sustained truncation selection) short enough that most
commercially fixed quality mutations in real crops — decades old — have almost
certainly passed this point long ago. Second, and less obvious, that the specific way a
breeding program *measures* the remaining polygenic background matters as much as which
prediction technology it adopts: decomposing a composite quality objective into its
true structural components and combining them with a proper index outperformed both a
single noisy holistic score and a genomic-prediction pipeline built on a realistic,
non-accumulating training population.

The comparatively modest performance of Arm C is not a general claim that genomic
selection underperforms index selection — it is specific to the training-population
design tested here (a fresh random half of the population phenotyped each generation,
with no historical accumulation, and moderate marker density relative to a 300-locus
background). Real published genomic-selection programs (e.g. Beyene et al. 2015)
achieve their largest advantage primarily through faster cycling — multiple genomic
selection cycles per calendar year — rather than necessarily higher per-cycle accuracy
than a well-designed phenotypic or index-based alternative; holding cycle count equal
across arms, as this simulation does, isolates exactly that per-cycle-accuracy
comparison and should not be read as a claim about calendar-time efficiency.

### Limitations

Sub-trait index weights in Arm B use the population's own true additive variances,
isolating the value of decomposition and optimal weighting from the separate,
real-world difficulty of estimating those weights from limited field data. Arm C
retrains from an independently sampled training set each generation; a program that
accumulates historical phenotypic and genotypic records, or that increases marker
density, would likely narrow or close the gap with Arm B. Population size, heritability
values, and QTL architecture were fixed a priori before any simulation was run; no
parameter was adjusted after inspecting results.
